AI in 2026

May 1, 2026

AIProduct

May 2026

If you'd asked me two years ago what AI in 2026 would look like, I would have guessed something dramatic. A new model with a name like a Greek god. A demo that broke the internet for a weekend. Some kind of inflection.

Instead, the most interesting thing that happened is that AI got boring. Not bad-boring. Infrastructure-boring. The way electricity is boring.

The chat window is still there, sure. People still type questions into it. But the chat window is no longer where the work happens. The work happens in the background, while you're doing something else, by an agent that knows what you asked for last Tuesday and doesn't need to be re-briefed every morning. The product isn't the conversation anymore. The product is the outcome.

That shift, more than any benchmark, is what 2026 actually feels like.

Three things that are different

Memory turned assistants into relationships. For most of the last decade, talking to an AI was like talking to a brilliant stranger with amnesia. You'd explain your project, your preferences, your weird workflow, and then close the tab and have to do it all over again the next day. That's gone. The assistants people actually use in 2026 remember. They have notes on you. They've watched you work. They know your kid's name and that you don't drink coffee after 2pm and that you always sign off "best" instead of "thanks." This is a smaller change than autonomy and a bigger one than benchmarks, and almost nobody is talking about it.

Agents work, mostly. The phrase "agentic AI" was insufferable in 2024. It pointed at something real. By the second half of 2025, the rough edges started to smooth out: longer-horizon tasks, computer use that didn't fall over the second a modal popped up, tools that actually composed instead of just being called in sequence. In 2026, "I had it draft the contract and send it to legal" is a thing a normal person says, and they mean it literally. The agent drafted the contract and sent it to legal. The interesting frontier is no longer "can it do the task" but "do I trust it enough to not check."

Cost collapsed, and so did the pretense. Inference is cheap. The frontier labs still charge frontier prices for frontier work, but the long tail of "good enough" has gotten so cheap it's basically free. This killed a lot of business models, freed up a lot of others, and forced everyone to be honest about what they were actually selling. Most "AI features" turned out to be wrappers. The ones that survived turned out to be products.

What's still hard

The remaining hard problems are the ones that were always hard, just dressed up. Trust. Liability. The seam where the agent hands off to the human and back again. What it means for someone to be "responsible" for an output that was 95% generated and 5% nudged. We don't have great answers, and the people pretending to are mostly selling something.

The other unsolved problem is taste. Models can write a competent blog post about AI in 2026. They can't tell you whether it's worth writing. That filter, the one that decides what deserves attention in the first place, is still firmly human. I suspect it will be for a while.

The boring conclusion

The story of AI in 2026 isn't "the singularity arrived." It's "the thing got useful." For people who wanted spectacle, that's a letdown. For everyone else, it's the better outcome. Tools become invisible when they work. Right now, finally, a lot of these tools are starting to disappear into the work itself.

Which, if you think about it, is the highest compliment you can pay to a piece of technology.
